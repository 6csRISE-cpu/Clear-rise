# Read this before you send anything

Every template in Unghosted is labeled with one of four **message types**. The type tells you what permission you need first. Get this right and your texts get delivered, your number stays off spam lists, and you stay clear of the laws that make careless texting expensive.

> **Why this matters:** in the U.S., text messages count as "calls" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA). Violations carry statutory damages of $500 per message, up to $1,500 if willful, and class-action lawyers actively look for businesses that text without permission. Several states have their own stricter versions ("mini-TCPAs"), and phone carriers enforce their own rules on top.

**How we wrote these rules:** where the law is unsettled or differs by state, we chose the **stricter** practice. Follow the *Operating rules* in each section and you'll usually be doing more than the minimum. This guide reflects federal rules and common state rules as of November 2026. It is practical guidance written for small businesses. It is not legal advice, and it can't account for every state. If you text at volume or across many states, have a TCPA attorney review your setup.

## The 4 message types at a glance

| Type | What it is | Examples | Permission you need first |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 · Reply** | Responding to a customer who contacted *you*, about *their* request | Missed-call text-back, answering their question, sending the quote they asked for | They contacted you. Reply only about what they asked |
| **2 · Service** | Messages about an existing job or a quote they requested | Appointment confirmations and reminders, "on my way," job updates, invoices, follow-ups on a quote they requested, a review request | Their **express permission** to text them about their job. Get it at booking or when giving the quote, and note it |
| **3 · Marketing** | Messages that try to sell something new, sent one at a time | Rebooking offers, seasonal promotions, maintenance-plan offers, referral asks, reviving an old quote months later | Their **written consent** to marketing texts, *before* you send |
| **4 · Automated marketing** | Marketing sent in bulk, on a schedule or by software | Campaign blasts, automated rebooking sequences, monthly promo texts | **Written consent** with full disclosures, a registered texting number, records you keep, and an opt-out that works automatically |

**The golden rule:** a message's type comes from its content, not from how friendly it sounds. If a reminder includes an offer ("…and 15% off a duct cleaning while we're there!"), it has become a marketing message. Keep Type 1 and Type 2 messages free of offers.

# Type 1 · Reply

**What it covers:** someone called, texted, emailed or filled in a form asking for help, and you're responding about *that* request.

**Why it's the safest type:** the customer started the conversation. Industry rules from wireless carriers (the CTIA Messaging Principles) treat customer-initiated conversations as having implied consent for replies.

**Operating rules**
1. **Stay on topic.** Reply only about what they asked for. No promotions, no "while I have you…"
2. **Keep it short and human.** One business, one person, one conversation.
3. **Three touches, then stop.** For missed calls: the first reply, one follow-up a few hours later, and one final message the next day. After that, the conversation is over unless they reply.
4. **Respect quiet hours** even here: no texts before **8 a.m.** or after **8 p.m.** in the customer's time zone. Federal law allows until 9 p.m. for telemarketing, but some states (including Florida, Maryland and Washington) stop at 8 p.m. Using 8 p.m. everywhere keeps it simple. *Exception: replying right away to someone who just contacted you about an emergency.*
5. **If they say stop, stop** (see *Opt-outs* below).

**Automating it?** Automatic missed-call text-back is fine for Type 1 *as long as the message stays responsive*: "Sorry I missed your call, what can I help with?" Your texting provider will still ask you to register your business number ("10DLC registration") and describe this use case. That's a one-time form.

# Type 2 · Service

**What it covers:** messages about a job the customer booked or a quote they requested: confirmations, reminders, arrival times, job-complete notes, invoices, quote follow-ups and review requests.

**What you need:** the customer's **express permission** to text them about their job. In practice, you get this when they book or when you give the quote.

**How to get it (pick one and do it every time):**
- **On your booking form or estimate:** a line like
  > "We'll text you about your appointment, your quote and your job at this number. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to opt out."
- **On the phone or in person**, then write it down: "Is it okay if I text you about the appointment and follow up on the quote?" → note "OK to text, {date}, {your initials}" in the customer record.

**Operating rules**
1. **Informational only.** Service messages stay about the job or the quote they asked for. No upsells inside them.
2. **Quote follow-ups have a shelf life.** Follow up on a quote they requested within about 3 weeks (5 touches maximum, as in our sequence). Reviving a quote **months later** is marketing (Type 3).
3. **Review requests are Type 2,** but follow Google's rules: ask *every* customer, use neutral wording, never offer anything in exchange (see the Review Engine in the Unghosted System).
4. **Quiet hours 8 a.m.–8 p.m.**, except time-sensitive job updates the customer expects (e.g., "running 20 minutes late").
5. **Honor opt-outs immediately.** If someone opts out, call or email them about their job instead.

# Type 3 · Marketing (one-to-one)

**What it covers:** any text that encourages someone to buy something they haven't already asked for: "your tune-up is due, want to book?", seasonal promos, maintenance-plan offers, referral requests, win-back messages, reviving an old quote.

**What you need:** the customer's **prior written consent** to receive marketing texts from your business, collected *before* the first marketing text.

> **Why written consent, even for one text sent by hand?** Federal law requires *prior express written consent* for marketing texts sent with an autodialer or artificial/prerecorded voice. Whether a particular texting app or field-service platform counts as an autodialer is still argued in court, and some state laws define automated systems more broadly. Separately, the wireless carriers expect written consent for promotional messages regardless of how they're sent. Collecting written consent once removes all of that doubt. It's the standard we follow, and the one we recommend.

**What counts as written consent:** a signed agreement, paper or electronic (a checkbox on a web or tablet form is fine), that clearly says:
- your business name,
- that they agree to receive **marketing** text messages at the number provided,
- that messages may be sent using automated technology,
- that consent is **not a condition of purchase**,
- message frequency, "Msg & data rates may apply," and how to opt out (STOP) and get help (HELP).

**Sample consent checkbox (unchecked by default):**
> ☐ Yes, I agree to receive marketing text messages from {Business} about seasonal maintenance, offers and service reminders at the number above. Messages may be sent using automated technology. Up to 4 msgs/month. Consent is not a condition of purchase. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to opt out, HELP for help. [Privacy policy link]

**Operating rules**
1. **No written consent, no marketing text.** Use email instead (see *Email* below) or a phone call, and ask for text consent next time you see them.
2. **The "existing customer" exception isn't enough on its own.** Federal do-not-call rules let you call someone who bought from you in the last 18 months (or inquired in the last 3) even if they're on the National Do Not Call Registry. That exception does **not** replace written consent for automated marketing texts, and some states limit it. Don't rely on it for texts.
3. **Quiet hours 8 a.m.–8 p.m.** recipient time. No exceptions for marketing.
4. **Frequency:** never more than **3 marketing messages to the same person in 24 hours** (a cap in several states). For a service business, 1–4 a month is plenty.
5. **Identify yourself** in every marketing text ("— {You}, {Business}") and include "Reply STOP to opt out" at least in the first message of each campaign.
6. **Keep records** of who consented, when, how and the exact wording they saw. Keep them for at least **5 years** (the federal lawsuit window is 4 years).

# Type 4 · Automated marketing

**What it covers:** marketing texts sent by software: bulk campaigns, scheduled sequences, automatic rebooking reminders, "blast to all customers" tools.

**What you need:** everything in Type 3, plus:
1. **A registered business number.** U.S. carriers require "10DLC" registration for business texting from standard numbers. Register a *marketing* campaign, describe how you collect consent, and give sample messages. Your texting provider handles the form. Unregistered or mis-described traffic gets blocked.
2. **An automatic opt-out.** Your tool must process STOP-type replies automatically and suppress that number from future sends.
3. **A consent record for every recipient** (date/time, source, wording, and IP address for web forms). Import only contacts with documented marketing consent into a marketing campaign. Don't upload your whole customer list.
4. **A clean list.** Remove numbers that opted out, bounced or were disconnected. For big lists, consider checking the FCC's Reassigned Numbers Database so you don't text a number that now belongs to someone else.
5. **The quiet-hours and frequency rules from Type 3**, set inside the tool (most tools have a send-window setting).

# Opt-outs: the rules for every type

**Treat any reasonable request to stop as an opt-out.** That includes STOP, STOPALL, END, CANCEL, UNSUBSCRIBE, QUIT, REVOKE, OPT OUT, and plain-English replies like "don't text me" or "take me off your list," as well as requests made by phone, email or in person.

**Operating rules**
1. **Stop immediately.** Federal rules give a maximum of 10 business days to honor a revocation. Don't use the window. Turn it off the same day.
2. **One confirmation, nothing else.** You may send a single confirmation ("You're unsubscribed from {Business} texts. Reply START to resubscribe."). No marketing in it. Send it within minutes or not at all.
3. **Stop all non-essential texts** to that person, not just the campaign they replied to. If you still need to reach them about a booked job, call or email.
4. **Write it down** in the customer record and in your texting tool, so no one on your team texts them again.
5. **Don't make opting out hard.** No "reply with your full name and address to unsubscribe."

> **2026 update to watch:** the FCC adopted further changes to its opt-out rules in late 2026, including letting businesses designate specific opt-out methods. Some parts take effect November 25, 2026, and others are still being phased in or are open for comment. Following the rules above (honor everything, immediately, everywhere) keeps you compliant under both the old and new versions. Ask your texting provider whether its opt-out handling is updated.

# Email: the simpler channel for marketing

U.S. marketing email (CAN-SPAM) doesn't require prior consent, which makes email the right channel for Type 3 messages to customers who haven't given text consent. Every marketing email must:
- have honest "From" and subject lines,
- include your **physical mailing address** (a PO box works),
- include a working **unsubscribe link**, honored within 10 business days (do it immediately),
- not be sent to anyone who unsubscribed.

*Customers in Canada:* Canada's anti-spam law (CASL) requires consent before most marketing emails and texts. Get express consent from Canadian customers.

# Quick reference card (print this)

| Before you send, ask… | If yes → |
|---|---|
| Is this a reply to something they just asked? | **Type 1.** Send. Stay on topic. Max 3 touches |
| Is it about a job they booked or a quote they asked for in the last ~3 weeks? | **Type 2.** Send if you have their OK to text. No offers |
| Does it try to sell them something new? | **Type 3.** Only with written consent. Otherwise email |
| Is software sending it to a list or on a schedule? | **Type 4.** Written consent + registered number + auto opt-out |
| Is it before 8 a.m. or after 8 p.m. their time? | **Wait** (unless it's an emergency reply or a time-sensitive job update) |
| Did they ever opt out? | **Don't text.** Call or email about their job only |
